What happens to wave propagation when the free surface is replaced by a thin elastic membrane? The short answer: everything changes — but in a way that can be derived exactly.

At **KAIST** (Graduate School of Mechanical Engineering, advised by Prof. Yeunwoo Cho), I studied gravity–capillary waves generated by a continuous air-jet impinging on a thin elastic silicone film floating above water. The film introduces a third phase — solid — between the gas and liquid, making this a true three-phase FSI problem.

**The key challenge** was that the standard dispersion relation for a free surface no longer applies: the film's bending stiffness and tension alter the restoring force at every wavenumber. I derived the dispersion relation for the gas–solid–liquid interface analytically, accounting for nonlinear elastic coupling, and identified the minimum phase speed (transition to Kelvin-wave regime) both theoretically and experimentally.

**What this taught me:** the boundary condition between fluid and solid is not a technicality — it is where the physics lives. The membrane was simultaneously a structural element and an interface, and the two roles could not be separated.

**Tools:** MATLAB · experimental wave tank · high-speed imaging
